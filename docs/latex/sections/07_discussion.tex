\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Learning Rate Controlled Everything}

Our results show a very clear pattern: the learning rate was the single most important factor in whether a model succeeded or failed. When we used $10^{-6}$, almost every model struggled. This makes sense when you consider that we kept the base convolutional layers frozen. Since we were starting the classification head from scratch, those weights needed a decent-sized "push" to start learning the features extracted by the pre-trained model. At $10^{-6}$, the updates were so tiny that the model barely moved from its initial random state before our \texttt{EarlyStopping} callback cut it off.

\subsection{Comparing the Optimizers}

It was interesting to see how the different optimizers handled the small dataset. RMSProp was the winner here, followed closely by Adam and Adamax. These adaptive methods are great for medical imaging projects like this because they can adjust their updates based on the gradients they see, which helps when the data is limited and potentially noisy.

On the flip side, AdaDelta was surprisingly poor. It's meant to be an improvement over Adagrad, but on our dataset, it seemed to "stall" almost immediately. We think this is because, with so few steps per epoch, AdaDelta's internal learning rate calculation dropped to zero before the model could find a good path. 

\subsection{Architecture Strengths}

ResNet50 was our most reliable performer across the board. We think its skip connections help keep the gradient signal clean as it passes back to the classification head. VGG19 actually gave us the single best individual run, which might be because its simpler, "old-school" architecture produces very straightforward features that our pooling-and-dense head could easily interpret. 

EfficientNetB3, which is the most modern model we tested, actually came in last on average. This was a bit of a surprise, but it's possible that its more complex feature space needs a lot more data than our 518 training images to really shine.

\subsection{A Warning about AUC}

One of the most important takeaways for us was about the AUC metric itself. We saw several "degenerate" runs where the model predicted the same class for every single image, yet they still had an AUC over 0.80. This happens because AUC measures how well the model *ranks* images, not how well it classifies them at a specific threshold. In a real clinical setting, a model that never finds a single cancer case is a total failure, even if its "ranking ability" looks good on paper. This reinforces the need to always check recall and specificity alongside AUC.

\subsection{What We'd Do Next}

If we were to continue this research, the next logical step would be fine-tuning. We kept the base models frozen to see what the "clean" transfer learning performance looked like, but unfreezing the last few layers and training them at a very low learning rate would likely boost the scores significantly. We'd also like to try $k$-fold cross-validation to get a better sense of how these models generalize across different subsets of the data.
